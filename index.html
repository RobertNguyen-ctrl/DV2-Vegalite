How active is Australia, really?

Nine in ten Australian adults do some form of sport or physical activity each year — but where they live, how old they are, and whether they're a man or a woman shape what they play far more than you might expect.

Why this matters

Physical inactivity is one of the largest preventable contributors to chronic disease in Australia, and governments spend hundreds of millions encouraging participation. But "being active" looks nothing like it did a generation ago — organised club sport is no longer the centre of the story.

This page is for an everyday Australian audience: anyone curious about how their own habits compare to the country's. No statistics knowledge assumed.

What you're looking at

Every chart is built from AusPlay, Australia's national physical-activity survey (~40,000 adults per year). For the map we combine it with ABS population estimates so a participation rate can be turned into an actual number of people.

Hover any chart for exact figures. The map is interactive — use the dropdown to switch measures, and click any state to see its top activities and barriers.

The geography of an active nation

Participation is high everywhere, but the small territories punch well above their weight.

The ACT and NT lead — but for opposite reasons

The ACT (91.3%) and Northern Territory (91.6%) top the nation, both well above the 85.3% national average. The ACT's lead is driven by income, education and cycle-friendly Canberra; the NT's by a young population and an outdoor lifestyle.

Switch the map to "active people" and the picture inverts: tiny territories shrink to almost nothing, while New South Wales alone accounts for ~7.2 million active adults — more than the next two states combined.

Key insight: A high participation rate and a large active population are different things. Policy that chases the headline percentage can overlook where the most people actually are.

02 — What

Walking won. Everything else is fighting for second.

Australia's most popular activities are overwhelmingly individual, informal and free.

The top of the table is unrecognisable from 20 years ago

Recreational walking (50.2%) is in a league of its own — done by half of all adults. Fitness/gym (34.1%) is second, followed by bush walking, running and swimming.

Notice what isn't near the top. Australia's iconic team sports — Australian football, cricket, netball — sit far down the list. The activities Australians actually do most are the ones that need no club, no fixture and no membership fee.

Key insight: The modern Australian "athlete" is a solo walker, not a weekend club player. Convenience and flexibility beat tradition.

03 — Who

Age and gender shape sport more than anything else

The same country contains very different sporting lives depending on who you are.

Every sport has a shape across the life course

The heatmap shows Gym & Fitness dominates at 15–24 but fades with age. Walking does the opposite — it grows continuously into the 60s. Golf is the only sport that simply keeps growing older with its players.

The butterfly shows the gender story is just as sharp. Pilates, yoga and walking lean strongly female; football, golf and cycling lean male. The activities closest to the centre — swimming, bush walking — are the ones that genuinely belong to everyone.

Key insight: There is no single “Australian participant.” Effective programs are age- and gender-specific, not one-size-fits-all.

04 — How

Informal beats organised — and it's growing

Most activity happens outside any club or competition, and that gap widens with age.

Clubs are no longer where activity lives

Among teenagers, organised and mixed settings still dominate. But by the time Australians reach their 50s and 60s, activity is overwhelmingly informal — done alone, on their own schedule, with no membership fee.

This creates a structural problem for sporting bodies: overall participation is up, but affiliated membership — and therefore revenue and influence — is stagnating or declining for most traditional sports.

Key insight: Participation is healthy, but it's draining out of clubs. That's a challenge for the bodies that depend on membership to survive.

05 — Changing

Who's rising, who's slipping

Rank shifts and year-on-year movement reveal where Australian activity is heading.

Fig 9 · Bump / rank-slope
How the top activities reshuffled, 2023/24 → 2025

Each line tracks one activity's rank across three survey rounds. Blue = climbed, coral = slipped, grey = held. Source: AusPlay 2023/24, 2024/25, 2025.

Fitness is surging; the old guard is drifting down

Fitness/gym (+3.8pp) and Pilates (+1.4pp) are the clear winners, climbing the rankings while traditional pursuits soften. Pickleball and padel nearly doubled — the fastest proportional growth of any activity.

Football, cycling and basketball all edged down. The bump chart shows these are part of a consistent direction: structured, scheduled sport is slowly giving ground to flexible fitness.

Key insight: The momentum is with low-commitment fitness and emerging social racket sports — exactly the activities that fit fragmented modern schedules.
06 — Why & why not

What pulls Australians in — and what holds them back

Motivations and barriers differ by gender in telling ways.

Where men and women agree on motivation and barriers — and where they don't

Try it: every dot is a reason — motivations (blue) and barriers (coral) — placed by how often men (x-axis) and women (y-axis) give it. Dots above the dashed line lean female; below lean male. Drag a box to select a cluster and the right-hand bar ranks just those reasons by overall share. Hover a dot to label it. Source: AusPlay 2025.

People want to move — life gets in the way

Physical health and fun are the top motivations for both men and women. Women cite mental health and weight management notably more. On the barrier side, the leading obstacles are poor health/injury and increasing age — not disinterest. Relatively few people simply "don't like sport."

Key insight: The motivation to be active is already there for most Australians. Removing practical barriers — accessible, low-cost, low-impact options — would do more than any campaign trying to convince people exercise is good for them.
